How this works

1What “Altitude” means

Altitude is a home-built 0–100% gauge for where the S&P 500 sits inside a rising “flight corridor” on a log scale — the floor is 0%, the ceiling is 100%, and the space between is a channel that drifts upward at a constant rate.

The corridor itself climbs 7.269%/yr, so holding altitude still requires the price to rise 7.269%/yr. A falling altitude does not mean the price is falling — only that it's rising slower than the channel.

t = year + (month-1)/12 + (day-1)/(days_in_month × 12) altitude = (log(price) − (AU + BU × t) + WIDTH) / WIDTH × 100 [%] AU = -133.011205444447 ceiling intercept (log) BU = 0.070173684970 ceiling slope (= 7.269%/yr) WIDTH = 1.487041168721 channel width (= 4.424×)

Where it sits historically

Since June 1932 (1,131 months), altitude has averaged 50.6%, median 50.0% — it clings to the middle of the channel. All-time high: 98.2% (Feb 1937). All-time low: 3.5% (Jul 1982).

2How the channel is drawn

Not a regression (no ±2σ band). The lines are tangents to the actual candle highs and lows, so overshoots and undershoots are captured directly rather than averaged away.

The ceiling connects the March 1937 high (18.67) and the March 2000 high (1,552.87) on a log scale, extended both ways. The floor runs parallel, dropped to touch the deepest low on record (102.20, Aug 1982).

Slope7.269%/yr (1.920 decades over 63 years)
Channel width4.424× (ceiling ÷ floor)
Floor → ceiling transit timewidth ÷ slope = 21 yr 2 mo

The striking part

From June 1932 to today — 1,131 months — no monthly high has crossed the ceiling. Excluding the two anchor points, the closest approaches were Feb 1937 (0.986×) and Apr 2000 (0.976×), measured against the ceiling on the actual date the high was set (the channel's time axis is anchored to month-start, so same-month measurements need the exact day). Checked against daily data too: across 23,669 trading days since 1932, the ceiling was crossed on zero days.

The floor is symmetric: the 2009-03-06 low sits at 1.0092× the floor on that date — the second-closest touch in 94 years.

But there's a circularity worth naming: a line drawn through the two highest points, by construction, cannot be crossed by those same two points. Zoom out past 1932 and it breaks immediately — August 1929 traded at 2.89× the extended ceiling, and January 1871 sits at 24.7×. The “never crossed” claim describes 1932–present only, and it's partly a property of how the line was drawn, not purely a discovery.

3What this can't do
Trading on this indicator has not made money in backtests. 30 rule variants were tested against 92 years of data. None beat buy-and-hold.

The cost of leaving the market

Since this only trades the S&P 500 itself, selling means parking in cash, and cash loses to inflation. To come out ahead, you'd need to call drawdowns worth more than 10.2 points a year, every year.

What was tested

30-strategy sweepSymmetric, buy-the-dip, and ceiling-only variants all failed. White's Reality Check p = 0.21–0.25 (i.e. not distinguishable from noise after correcting for having tried 30 strategies)
Original ruleFired only once in 62 years. After that sale (May 1997), the price rose another 78% before the eventual top
Walk-forwardRe-drawing the channel with only past data at each point exits the market in Jul 1987 and doesn't re-enter until Jul 2002 — sitting out one of the largest bull runs on record
Is the parallelism real?Against random log-walks with the same drift and volatility, 27% produce equally parallel tangents by chance (p = 0.270) — because in a long random walk both tangents converge on the drift rate regardless
Drawdown controlWorst drawdown −50.1% vs. buy-and-hold's −51.8%. Since the rule buys back 100% at the 0% line, it always takes the next crash fully invested

This is a map, not a trading signal. Use it as one, and you're taking on open-ended upside risk in exchange for a hurdle rate you have to clear every single year.

Peak altitude has essentially no predictive value for drawdown depth: the correlation across all 15 episodes is r = −0.119. The Dot-com Crash started at the ceiling (100.0%) and fell 50.5%; the 2008 Global Financial Crisis — the deepest of the group at −57.7% — started from an unremarkable 65.2%. The single episode that reached the floor (0.0%) started from 30.4%, the lowest starting altitude of the 15. The 1929 crash (−86.2% peak-to-trough) is excluded: its peak sits outside the channel's valid range.

5Is the altitude distribution actually normal?

Take the 1,131 monthly closes since June 1932 and histogram them. The shape is close to a bell curve: mean 50.58%, median 50.02%, skewness +0.04 (essentially symmetric), excess kurtosis −0.34 (slightly thinner tails than normal — no month has ever landed below 0% or above 100%, by construction).

Two of four normality tests reject the null (Shapiro–Wilk p = 7×10⁻⁵, Anderson–Darling stat 1.18 vs. a 0.75 critical value); two don't (Kolmogorov–Smirnov p = 0.51, Jarque–Bera p = 0.06). What they're detecting is the thin tails, not outliers.

The 1,131 observations are not 1,131 independent data points. Monthly altitude has an AR(1) autocorrelation of 0.9844, which works out to an effective sample size of about 8.9. Treat every statistic on this page with that in mind — a smooth-looking histogram here is mostly a smooth-looking random walk, not a large sample.
